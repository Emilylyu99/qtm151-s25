1. Identifying the "Rights Paradox": Countries Strong in Freedoms but Weak in Basic Needs¶

In this section, we process and integrate data on Civil and Political Rights (CPR) and Economic and Social Rights (ESR) to identify a phenomenon referred to as the "Rights Paradox"—where countries perform strongly in civil liberties but poorly in basic social and economic provisions.

First, we calculate an overall CPR score by averaging the existing physical integrity and empowerment scores from the dataset. For ESR, I compute a score separately for low/middle-income and high-income countries by averaging five core indicators: education, food, health, housing, and work. These ESR scores are then combined and merged with the CPR scores by matching countries and years.

Next, we calculate the rights gap as the difference between the CPR and ESR scores. To identify paradoxical cases, we define countries in the top 25% of CPR scores and bottom 25% of ESR scores as exhibiting this mismatch. These are the countries where people may enjoy strong protections of freedom and safety, yet lack access to fundamental needs like healthcare or education.

Finally, we output a list of the top countries with the largest rights gaps to spotlight the most extreme instances of this paradox.

3. Trend Analysis of the Rights Gap Over Time (2017–2023)¶

In this section, we analyze how the mismatch between civil and political rights (CPR) and economic and social rights (ESR)—referred to as the "rights gap"—has evolved from 2017 to 2023.

First, we calculate the average rights gap for each year by grouping the dataset by year and computing the mean difference between CPR and ESR scores. Then, we create a line plot to visualize the yearly trend of this gap. To help identify the overall direction of change, we fit a linear regression line to the plot. This red dashed trendline indicates whether the gap is increasing or decreasing over time, providing insights into whether countries are becoming more or less balanced in their protection of both types of rights.

📉 Key Observation: Declining Balance Between Rights Over Time¶

From 2017 to 2021, the average rights gap (CPR - ESR) has shown a gradual widening, as indicated by the downward-sloping trendline with a slope of −0.4504. This suggests that, on average, countries have become less balanced in their protection of civil/political rights versus economic/social rights—either improving CPR without equivalent ESR progress, or experiencing greater ESR shortfalls over time. The sharpest decline occurred between 2019 and 2020, highlighting a possible period of increased rights disparity.

Insight 2: Structural Typology of Human Rights¶

Q: Can countries be systematically classified into distinct structural profiles of human rights performance—based on CPR, ESR, gender disparities, and group-specific vulnerabilities? What patterns emerge?¶

We will three sets of indicators to explore human rights performance across countries:

  • CPR Score (Civil and Political Rights): Average of empowerment and physical integrity scores.
  • ESR Score (Economic and Social Rights): Average achievement in rights to health, education, housing, work, and food.
  • Group Risk: Proportion of marginalized groups (e.g., LGBTQ+, women, immigrants) facing elevated risk in freedom of expression and related areas.

Using data from 2017 to 2023, we applied PCA and KMeans clustering to classify countries into distinct human rights structures based on these indicators.

First, we integrates data from three sources (cpr, esr_combined, and people_risk) to create a unified dataset for analysis, which can be used for further statistical or machine learning tasks.

Overall, the results highlight the critical role of gender disparities in explaining rights misalignment, while group risks may require further investigation or additional data to clarify their impact.

Key Explanations for Inequality:¶

  1. Structural Inequalities in Resource Allocation: Economic and social rights require significant investment in infrastructure and services, which may be deprioritized in favor of civil and political rights due to limited resources. Gender disparities often arise from systemic biases in resource distribution.

  2. Cultural and Social Norms: Deeply ingrained societal norms perpetuate gender inequality and discrimination against marginalized groups, restricting their access to education, healthcare, and economic opportunities, even in countries with strong civil and political rights.

  3. Policy and Governance Gaps: Governments may focus on civil and political rights for international recognition while neglecting economic and social rights. Weak enforcement of policies aimed at reducing gender disparities and protecting marginalized groups exacerbates inequalities.
